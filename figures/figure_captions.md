Generated by `notebooks/03_visualizations.ipynb`; every number below is computed in that notebook.

## fig01_gaps_and_missingness.png

Data problems are concentrated in a few columns. The worst is `expected_attendance` in events_calendar.csv (32.7% of rows), and every other trip-table column is below 2.0%. The timeline separates three kinds of gap: one platform outage (42 h on 12 May, 13 May) hitting every zone, Ayat having no rows until it launched on 15 Mar, and scattered single missing hours. None of these gaps are filled with zero demand.

## fig02_before_after_cleaning.png

Cleaning changes the picture for three variables. 353 temperature readings in the 50–80 range were Fahrenheit and become °C; 76 rain readings coded −9999 pulled monthly mean rain as low as -187 mm/h and are interpolated instead; 1.0% blank and 0.8% negative (−1) trip counts are imputed. Without these fixes the rain and temperature features would be meaningless.

## fig03_demand_trend_with_holidays.png

City demand grows steadily through the year: the linear trend rises 61% from January to October (about 87 more trips per day each week), so a November forecast needs a trend or recent-level term. 13 of the 13 public holidays fall below their 7-day mean, so holidays matter too, but they are short dips on top of the trend rather than a change in it.

## fig04_hour_by_weekday_heatmap.png

City-wide demand peaks on Fri at 18:00. The zones contrast sharply: Arat Kilo's weekend demand is 0.49× its weekday level, while Bole's is 1.31×, with late-night weekend hours. A single city-wide weekly profile would be wrong for both, so the model needs zone × hour × day-of-week interactions.

## fig05_zone_profiles.png

Each zone type has its own daily shape. Weekday peaks fall at business 18:00, market 13:00, nightlife/airport 06:00, residential 07:00, transport/hub 18:00. At weekends, business zones flatten while the nightlife/airport zone keeps its demand into the night, which is why zone type (or zone) has to interact with hour and weekend in the model.

## fig06_weather_timezone_check.png

Weather timestamps ending in 'Z' are UTC. Read as local time, temperature would peak at 12:00; after the +3 h conversion it peaks at 15:00, matching the slash-format rows (already local, peak 13:00). The rain effect on demand is strongest on the corrected clock (×1.27) and drops to ×1.11 if UTC is left unconverted, so the conversion directly protects the rain features.

## fig07_rain_effect.png

Rain increases demand and the effect grows with intensity, but not equally across zone types: in heavy rain the uplift ranges from ×0.69 (market) to ×1.54 (residential). Heavy-rain hours are rare (113 city hours), so a rain-class × zone-type interaction should be learned with regularisation.

## fig08_event_study.png

Events lift demand in their own zone, but the timing differs by type. Peak uplift: football match ×2.21 at +3 h; concert ×1.90 at +5 h; conference ×1.29 at +0 h; sports run ×1.28 at -1 h. The 7-days-earlier comparison line stays near 1, so the lift comes from the event, not the time slot. This is why the event features use a separate window per type (before / during / after) rather than one flag.

## fig09_holiday_effects.png

Holidays do not all reduce demand. 9 of 13 fall below normal, the deepest being Genna (index 0.73); 1 sit above normal (highest: Adwa Victory Day, 1.05). The whiskers show ordinary day-to-day variation, so only bars that clear them are real effects. The size and direction of the effect depend on the holiday, so a holiday-specific feature would beat a single yes/no flag.

## fig10_model_comparison.png

hist gradient boosting is the best model on the 18–31 Oct validation fortnight: RMSE 13.61 trips per zone-hour, versus 15.52 for the seasonal-naive baseline and 29.47 for the mean baseline (12% better than seasonal naive). Across 4 rolling-origin folds it averages 14.93 ± 2.05, against 16.77 ± 1.67 for seasonal naive, so the advantage holds in every period tested, not just one.

## fig11_forecast_vs_actual.png

The hist gradient boosting forecast tracks the daily cycle and the weekday/weekend switch in all three zones (Merkato RMSE 29.1; Bole RMSE 11.4; Arat Kilo RMSE 7.5 trips per hour). The largest misses are the sharpest one-off peaks, which neither the model nor the seasonal-naive baseline anticipates, consistent with unlisted events or bursts in the history.

## fig12_feature_importance.png

Recent demand level and the zone/hour structure carry most of the signal (top feature: `lag_wk_mean`, +13.11 RMSE when shuffled). Both joined tables still contribute: the strongest weather feature is `temp_c` (+0.31) and the strongest event feature is `event_attendance` (+0.24). These matter mainly in the rare rainy or event hours, where errors are largest.
